01 / FIELD NOTES

A blockchain is a replicated state machine

Multiple nodes independently check the same ordered transitions.

Think of a ledger as a current state plus rules for changing it. A transaction requests a transition; a block groups ordered requests; nodes execute validation rules and update their own copy. Hash-linked blocks help commit to history, while consensus decides which competing history participants accept.

Signatures alone cannot prevent the same coin from being spent in two conflicting requests. A shared ordering and validity rule must resolve the conflict. A centralized database can also enforce ordering; a public blockchain addresses the harder setting where no single writer is automatically trusted.

03 / FIELD NOTES

Choose a blockchain when its trust model is useful

Replication, public verification and open access come with costs.

A blockchain can enable participants who do not share one administrator to verify a common ledger. It usually adds execution cost, limited throughput, exposed metadata and difficult recovery. A hash on a chain proves a commitment was recorded; it does not prove that an uploaded invoice, sensor reading or real-world title was truthful.

Permissioned networks choose an allowed validator set; permissionless networks allow participation under protocol rules. “Distributed” does not automatically mean decentralized governance or equal influence. Identify the actual validators, upgrade authority and access dependencies.
